# Making a decent YouTube video: evidence for spoken-text feedback and cut proposals

Web research, read-only, done on **2026-10-10**. It collects what official YouTube documentation, research on
instructional video and on speech, and a few well-regarded practitioners say about hooks, retention, length,
chapters, end screens, cards, audio and tutorials, and above all about the spoken text. Purpose: let a coding
agent that edits a video from its transcript (1) give feedback on what was said and (2) weigh that guidance
when proposing cuts. Target: Italian, one speaker, landscape screencasts with a corner webcam overlay that demo
the user's own software and AI coding agents at work, 5–15 minutes.

Conventions: **[Y#]** official YouTube or Google page; **[R#]** peer-reviewed or conference research (DOI
given); **[P#]** practitioner, vendor or press material: **secondary**, it informs but proves nothing alone.
*Heuristic* marks a number proposed here, not taken from a source. Sources are paraphrased. Limits: the Guo and
Kim 2014 PDFs could not be parsed (findings from their abstracts and from [R3]); some effect sizes come from
abstracts or secondary summaries (marked); X/Twitter, BBC and the original MrBeast PDF could not be fetched. No
primary Creator Insider or YouTube Liaison statement on intros or length was found; second-hand ones are in §6.

## 1. Official YouTube guidance

### 1.1 Retention report and the intro
- [Y1] [Measure key moments for audience retention](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415): flat
  line = watched through; gradual decline = fading interest; spike = rewatched or shared; dip = viewers leave
  or skip there; top moment = almost nobody left. **Intro** = share still watching after the first 30 s; a high
  intro can mean the opening matched what title and thumbnail made viewers expect. Remedies listed: title and
  thumbnail that reflect the content, and a reworked first 30 s (try styles). Late top moments: move compelling
  material earlier, as the audience shrinks along the video; review dips. Typical retention = the channel's 10
  latest videos of similar length. Key moments need a video of at least 60 s and 100 views.
- [Y2] [Content tab analytics tips](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12942217): a typical intro lasts
  about 30 s; videos keeping at least 50 % of viewers at 30 s enter the "above typical intros" list. No typical
  value is published. Compare only videos of the same format.
- [Y3] [Understand your content performance for YouTube's recommendation system](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16559650):
  viewers decide in the first seconds; keep the intro short, go straight to the value and deliver what title
  and thumbnail promised; experiment with storytelling, structure, editing and delivery; enjoyment counts.
- [Y4] [Understand your YouTube content performance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281): since
  24 Aug 2026 a view counts when playback starts; *engaged views* count viewers who stayed past the first
  few seconds. Views no longer reflect the opening; retention and engaged views do.

### 1.2 Keeping the promise of title and thumbnail
- [Y5] Cristos Goodrow, [On YouTube's recommendation system](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/),
  2021-09-15: in 2011 YouTube learned that a click does not mean the video was watched, and added watch time;
  not all watch time is equal, so viewers are surveyed (1–5 stars) and only 4–5-star views count as *valued
  watch time*.
- [Y6] [Spam, deceptive practices and scams policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973):
  *malicious clickbait* (packaging that tricks viewers into a video that does not deliver it) is prohibited.
- [Y7] [YouTube Creators: Optimize your content](https://www.youtube.com/creators/grow/optimize-your-content/):
  titles should describe the video accurately and get to the point; thumbnails simple and readable on every
  device; several titles and thumbnails can be tested.

### 1.3 Hooks and intros: what YouTube has said over time
- [Y8] Ryan Nugent, [Creator Playbook tips: the first 15 seconds](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/youtube-creator-playbook-tips-first-15),
  2011-08-27: open with something that grabs attention (words or picture); make the topic clear early; tease
  what is coming; put channel branding later; in a how-to, first show a sneak peek of the finished result.
- [Y9] [Four tips to hook your viewers](https://blog.youtube/creator-and-artist-stories/four-tips-to-hook-your-viewers-on/),
  2014-06-24: start with a question; brand strongly but as part of the content; tell viewers what they will
  see (topic clear within about 5 s); or cold-open straight into the action.
- [Y10] [YouTube Creators: Longform](https://www.youtube.com/creators/create/longform/): hook in the first
  5–10 s; outline a beginning, middle and end; talk as if explaining something to a friend; record somewhere
  quiet and well lit; plan the edit while writing. The windows differ (5–10 s, 15 s, 30 s) with the documents'
  age and purpose; the 30 s of [Y1] is a measurement window, not a recommended intro length.

### 1.4 Length, chapters, end screens, cards
- Length: no official page read recommends one; [Y10] gives only upload limits (15 min by default, more for
  verified accounts) and [Y1]/[Y2] compare retention among videos of similar length. Staff quotes: §6.
- [Y11] [Video chapters](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9884579): timestamps and titles in the
  description; first timestamp 00:00; at least three timestamps in ascending order; each chapter at least
  10 s. Automatic chapters are on by default; manual timestamps override them; a note excludes channels with
  active strikes. [Y12] Google Search can show the timestamps as *key moments*
  ([Search Central](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/video)), unrelated to retention.
- [Y13] [Add end screens](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6388789): only in the last 5–20 s; video at
  least 25 s; up to 4 elements in 16:9; external links only in the Partner Program; viewers can hide them;
  leave room and time for them and consider the last 20 s when editing; none on made-for-kids videos.
- [Y14] [Add cards](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6140493): up to 5 cards per video (video,
  playlist, channel; links need the Partner Program); the teaser shows at the time you set.

### 1.5 Audio, captions, tutorials, devices
- Official guidance on voice quality is thin: [Y10] says record somewhere quiet; [Y15]
  [upload encoding settings](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171) give only specs (48 kHz,
  AAC-LC or Opus, stereo). [Y16] [Automatic captioning](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6373554) goes
  wrong with mispronunciation, accents, noise, poor sound or overlapping speech; Italian is supported.
- Tutorials: the only specific official advice found is the sneak peek [Y8] and saying early what comes [Y9].
- [Y17] Neal Mohan, [Our big bets for 2025](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/our-big-bets-for-2025/),
  2025-02-11: in the US, TV has overtaken mobile as the main YouTube device by watch time (over 1 billion
  hours a day). No Italian figure found. Screen text must survive a phone and a TV across the room.

## 2. Research evidence

### 2.1 Engagement with instructional video
- [R1] Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014, L@S, [doi:10.1145/2556325.2566239](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566239)
  ([abstract](https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.txt)): 6.9 M
  edX sessions; engagement = time watched and attempting the follow-up problem. Shorter videos were much more
  engaging, as were informal talking-head and Khan-style tablet-drawing videos; even good recorded classroom
  lectures were not; lectures and tutorials are watched differently. Per [R3]: median engagement near 100 %
  under 6 min, ~50 % at 9–12 min, ~20 % at 12–40 min; it rose with speaking rate. Correlational, MOOC learners.
- [R2] Kim, Guo et al. 2014, L@S, [doi:10.1145/2556325.2566237](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566237)
  ([abstract](https://pg.ucsd.edu/publications/edX-MOOC-in-video-dropouts-peaks_LAS-2014.txt)): more dropout in
  longer videos, re-watching sessions and tutorials; 61 % of interaction peaks (80 sampled videos) fell on visual
  transitions; peaks came from starting new material, returning to missed content, following a tutorial step,
  replaying a short segment, or re-hearing a non-visual explanation.
- [R3] Brame 2016, CBE—Life Sci Educ, [doi:10.1187/cbe.16-03-0125](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.16-03-0125):
  review; keep videos short, signal, segment, weed out extras (music, busy backgrounds), pair narration with
  complementary visuals, talk conversationally, fairly fast and with enthusiasm, give learner control
  (labelled chapters), add guiding questions.
- [R4] Lagerstrom, Johanes & Ponsukcharoen 2015, ASEE, [doi:10.18260/p.24895](https://doi.org/10.18260/p.24895):
  in two Stanford online CS courses (working professionals) ~90 % watched whole 50–75-min lecture videos, over
  several sessions; median session 12–13 min (per a [UCSD summary](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/video-length.html)).

### 2.2 Multimedia learning principles applied to screencasts
- Signaling: [R5] Schneider et al. 2018, Educ Res Rev, [doi:10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001):
  103 studies, N = 12,201; cues help retention strongly and transfer moderately (secondary summary's labels).
  [R6] Mautone & Mayer 2001, J Educ Psychol, [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.377](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.377):
  previews, headings and pointer words improved transfer. In a screencast: spoken signposts, highlights, zooms.
- Segmenting: [R7] Rey et al. 2019, Educ Psychol Rev, [doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4):
  56 investigations; small-to-medium gains in retention and transfer and lower cognitive load, also with
  system-paced segments. In a screencast: clear step boundaries, chapters.
- Coherence: [R8] Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, Educ Psychol Rev, [doi:10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4):
  interesting but irrelevant details hurt learning (g ≈ −0.33, 58 studies, secondary summary). [R9] Moreno &
  Mayer 2000, J Educ Psychol, [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.117](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.117):
  background music and sounds added to narrated instruction hurt learning.
- Personalization: [R10] Ginns, Martin & Marsh 2013, Educ Psychol Rev, [doi:10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9228-0):
  conversational style improved retention (d = 0.30) and transfer (d = 0.54); small, non-significant in
  studies longer than 35 min.

### 2.3 Delivery: pace, energy, fluency, fillers, pauses
- Pace: engagement rose with speaking rate in [R1]. [R11] Murphy et al. 2022, Appl Cogn Psychol,
  [doi:10.1002/acp.3899](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.3899): lecture comprehension barely changed at 1.5× or 2×
  playback and fell beyond 2×. [R12] Coupé et al. 2019, Sci Adv, [doi:10.1126/sciadv.aaw2594](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aaw2594):
  17 languages, Italian included, differ in syllable rate and information per syllable but converge near
  39 bits/s, so English words-per-minute targets do not carry over to Italian.
- Energy: [R13] Horovitz & Mayer 2021, Comput Hum Behav, [doi:10.1016/j.chb.2021.106724](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2021.106724):
  viewers recognise and catch the instructor's emotion; a happy instructor raised motivation, not test scores.
- Fluency: [R14] Carpenter et al. 2013, Psychon Bull Rev, [doi:10.3758/s13423-013-0442-z](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0442-z):
  a fluent delivery (no notes, eye contact) earned better ratings and more perceived learning, with equal
  learning. On YouTube, perception is what drives staying and rating [Y5].
- Fillers: [R15] Fraundorf & Watson 2011, J Mem Lang, [doi:10.1016/j.jml.2011.03.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2011.03.004):
  uh/um in told stories improved recall versus coughs of equal length; a web replication (Muhlack et al. 2021,
  [Interspeech](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2021/muhlack21_interspeech.html)) found the opposite in
  German and no effect in English. [R16] Niebuhr & Fischer 2019, [Interspeech](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2019/niebuhr19c_interspeech.html):
  the shorter (and more nasal) a presenter's filled pauses, the more listeners underestimated their number and
  the better they rated the talk. No Italian data found.
- Pauses: [R17] MacGregor, Corley & Donaldson 2010, Neuropsychologia, [doi:10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2010.09.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2010.09.024):
  words heard after a silent pause were recognised more often later.

### 2.4 Structure: problem first, questions, concreteness
- [R18] Muller et al. 2008, J Comput Assist Learn, [doi:10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2007.00248.x):
  364 physics students; videos that stated and refuted common misconceptions beat a clear exposition (effect
  sizes 0.79 and 0.83). First author: Derek Muller of Veritasium.
- [R19] Carpenter & Toftness 2017, J Appl Res Mem Cogn, [doi:10.1016/j.jarmac.2016.07.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jarmac.2016.07.014):
  questions put before short video segments improved learning, also of material not asked about; a follow-up
  with lectures over 20 min found smaller effects.
- [R20] Loewenstein 1994, Psychol Bull, [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.116.1.75):
  curiosity as an information gap; Kang et al. 2009, Psychol Sci, [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02402.x):
  curiosity improved memory for the answers.
- [R21] Sadoski, Goetz & Rodriguez 2000, J Educ Psychol, [doi:10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.92.1.85):
  concreteness closely tracked how comprehensible, interesting and memorable texts were (written texts;
  known from summaries only).

### 2.5 Credibility: audio, hedges, apologies
- [R22] Newman & Schwarz 2018, Sci Commun, [doi:10.1177/1075547018759345](https://doi.org/10.1177/1075547018759345):
  the same talks with degraded audio made researchers seem less intelligent and likeable, their work less important.
- [R23] Blankenship & Holtgraves 2005, J Lang Soc Psychol, [doi:10.1177/0261927X04273034](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X04273034):
  hedges, hesitations and tag questions lowered persuasion and source ratings when the topic mattered. Durik et
  al. 2008, [doi:10.1177/0261927X08317947](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X08317947): hedges hurt only
  sometimes, by type and placement. Jensen 2008, [doi:10.1111/j.1468-2958.2008.00324.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2008.00324.x):
  reporting genuine uncertainty made scientists and journalists more trusted.

### 2.6 Webcam overlay, cuts, Italian discourse markers
- [R24] Alemdag 2022, Educ Inf Technol, [doi:10.1007/s10639-022-11154-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10639-022-11154-w):
  20 experiments; an on-screen instructor did not significantly change learning or social presence and raised
  motivation and cognitive load. Kizilcec, Bailenson & Gomez 2015, [doi:10.1037/edu0000013](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000013):
  field studies (N = 12,468): constant face vs face at chosen moments made no difference to learning or attrition.
- [R25] Smith & Henderson 2008, J Eye Mov Res, [doi:10.16910/jemr.2.2.6](https://doi.org/10.16910/jemr.2.2.6):
  viewers asked to spot edits in film clips missed about a quarter of cuts between two views of one scene, a
  third when the cut fell on a motion onset; continuity-style cuts were missed most. No study was found that tests jump cuts in instructional or screencast video.
- [R26] Carla Bazzanella, [Segnali discorsivi](<https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/segnali-discorsivi_(Enciclopedia-dell'Italiano)/>),
  Enciclopedia dell'Italiano, Treccani 2011: markers serve interactional, metatextual and cognitive functions;
  *cioè* mainly introduces a reformulation, *diciamo* can soften or strengthen, *allora* takes the turn, marks
  transitions or conclusions, or fills; little propositional content, real pragmatic value; habits go unnoticed.

## 3. Practitioner guidance (secondary)

- [P1] Paddy Galloway (YouTube strategist), [post on X](https://x.com/PaddyG96/status/1788967959882346991),
  about 2024-05-10 (seen through a search index): plan title and thumbnail before recording; don't waste the
  viewer's time in the intro, get to the value at once; the best intro doesn't feel like one but flows on.
- [P2] *How to Succeed in MrBeast Production*, onboarding document leaked in 2024 (about 36 pages, attributed
  to Jimmy Donaldson, unconfirmed: [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2024/09/17/mrbeast-internal-production-guide-leaked-key-points/);
  read via a [transcription](https://www.alexanderjarvis.com/memo-how-to-succeed-in-mrbeast-production/)): the
  first minute matters most and must match title and thumbnail; then stop announcing and show; re-engage near
  minutes 3 and 6; no dull moments; keep it simple; end right after a satisfying payoff, unannounced.
- [P3] Veritasium (Derek Muller), [Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2xHZPH5Sng),
  2021 (via a [Gigazine summary](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20210830-clickbait-effective/)): packaging
  takes as much effort as editing; enticing but accurate packaging ("legitbait") is fine, deception draws
  backlash; name the topic in words that people who don't know it yet would recognise.
- [P4] John Lindquist (egghead.io), [Recording a great coding screencast](https://dev.to/egghead/recording-a-great-coding-screencast-g35),
  2020-10-23: start at once (no greeting, no list of what the lesson covers) and end fast; never talk without
  showing or show without talking; monotone kills a lesson; record with pauses and mistakes, then ripple-delete
  them; code is the largest element (80–120-character column, font enlarged to fit); margin for player controls;
  even audio levels.
- [P5] Scott Hanselman, [The guide to creating quality technical screencasts](https://www.hanselman.com/blog/the-guide-to-creating-quality-technical-screencasts),
  2011-07-19: large fonts (Consolas 15 at 1280×720, readable on a phone); zoom and highlight in the edit; decent
  mic, pop filter, mind keyboard clicks; avoid long silences; don't apologise or make excuses for mistakes.
- [P6] Jeff Geerling, [forum post](https://community.amperecomputing.com/t/review-of-the-ampere-developer-platform-by-jeff-geerling/277),
  2023-04-21: finishing the script he usually cuts deep technical details that make most general viewers
  leave, and publishes them on his blog or in issues instead.
- [P7] Il Post, [Allora, ehm, parliamo, cioè, di parole riempitive](https://www.ilpost.it/2026/01/27/parole-riempitive-filler-words-marcatori-del-discorso/),
  2026-01-27 (press quoting linguists): markers manage conversation and are not useless, but careless overuse
  hampers understanding; Vera Gheno suggests accepting short silences instead of filling them.
- [P8] Wistia, [Understanding audience retention](https://wistia.com/learn/marketing/understanding-audience-retention),
  2020-01-03 (vendor data, website business videos): early drop 4.9 % at 1–2 min, 17.3 % at 5–10 min; the
  drop at the end is largest for 10–20-min videos.
- [P9] UBC wiki, [Writing for the ear](https://wiki.ubc.ca/Course:LFS400/Workshops/Writing_For_the_Ear), 2021:
  one thought per sentence; a sentence should fit in one normal breath; find long ones by reading aloud.

## 4. Checklist for feedback on the spoken text

Apply to the transcript with word timestamps; report each hit with timestamp, words, check number and a
proposed rewording or cut. Italian examples are illustrative.

| # | Check | Why (one line) | Sources | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the problem or promise stated within the first 20 s (ideally 10), in words that echo the title and thumbnail? | Viewers decide in the first seconds (official windows 5–10 s to 30 s); the 30-s intro measures whether the opening matched the packaging | Y1, Y2, Y3, Y10, P1, P2 | any language |
| 2 | In a demo, is the end result shown or concretely described before the build starts ("alla fine avremo X che fa Y")? | Official sneak-peek advice for tutorials; a preview is a signal | Y8, Y9, R6 | any language |
| 3 | Does anything delay the promise: greeting, self-introduction or channel pitch longer than one short sentence, or setup chatter ("allora… vediamo… ok, partiamo")? | Branding later; intro concise; the best intro does not feel like one | Y3, Y8, P1, P4 | any language |
| 4 | Is meta-talk ("in questo video vedremo…", "prima di iniziare…", "ci arriviamo dopo") one concrete sentence, not an agenda of 3+ items or a recurring promise? | A short concrete preview helps; a generic agenda delays the value; show rather than announce | R6, Y9, P4, P2 | any language |
| 5 | Is the question the demo answers (or the obvious wrong approach) posed before the solution? | Questions and refuted misconceptions raise attention and learning | Y9, R18, R19, R20 | any language |
| 6 | Is each claim tied to something concrete on screen (named file, command, number, visible output) rather than "è potentissimo", "fa cose incredibili"? | Concreteness aids comprehension; talk and show together | R21, R3, P4 | any language |
| 7 | When the speaker points ("qui", "questo", "come vedete"), is the referent named (line, file, panel)? | Pointer words with a named target guide attention and give the edit a zoom target | R5, R6, P5 | any language |
| 8 | Can each sentence be said in one breath, one idea per sentence? Flag sentences over ~25 words or with stacked subordinate clauses (*heuristic*). | Listeners cannot re-read | P9 | any language |
| 9 | At each topic change, is there a short spoken signpost ("Primo passo: …", "Ora che X funziona, passiamo a Y") usable as a chapter title? | Verbal signals and segmenting help; chapters need ≥3 parts of ≥10 s | R6, R7, Y11 | any language |
| 10 | Are filled pauses and markers (ehm, cioè, praticamente, diciamo, tipo, allora, quindi, niente, ecco, insomma, vabbè) clustered, long, or repeated as tics? Leave markers that do work (cioè before a real reformulation, quindi for a consequence, a deliberate diciamo). | Long, frequent fillers lower perceived performance; markers have functions; recall evidence is mixed | R16, R15, R14, R26, P7 | inventory Italian; principle any language |
| 11 | Are there apologies or excuses that add nothing ("scusate il casino", "non so se si vede", "non sono un esperto", "sono un po' stanco")? | Powerless markers cost credibility; don't apologise for mistakes | R23, P5 | any language |
| 12 | Are hedges ("forse", "in teoria", "dovrebbe", "un po'") attached to things the speaker knows? Keep one explicit caveat where uncertainty is real (e.g. the agent is not deterministic). | Habitual hedging weakens the message; stated real uncertainty can raise trust | R23 | any language |
| 13 | Is the viewer addressed directly and conversationally ("proviamo", "vedi/vedete") rather than impersonally ("si procede a…", "l'utente deve…"), consistently with tu or voi? | Conversational style improves learning; talk as to a friend | R10, Y10 | principle any language; tu/voi is an unstudied Italian style choice |
| 14 | Does energy sag in long stretches (trailing sentences, "vabbè", "niente", flat narration; speech rate far below the speaker's own median)? | Enthusiasm and pace go with engagement; monotone kills lessons | R1, R13, P4 | any language; weak signal in a transcript |
| 15 | During agent waits, is the talk useful (what the agent is doing, what to watch for) or filler ("aspettiamo… un attimo…")? | No dull moments; filler waits are speed-up or cut candidates | P2, P5, R8 | any language |
| 16 | Does each digression serve the promise? Flag tangents over ~30 s (*heuristic*) that do not lead to the payoff; suggest the description or another video. | Irrelevant details hurt; practitioners cut deep tangents | R8, P6, Y1 | any language |
| 17 | Is the payoff shown and stated ("ecco, adesso X funziona") and not buried after unrelated material? If the strongest moment comes late, could it be previewed early? | The promise must be delivered; YouTube advises moving compelling content earlier | Y1, Y3, P2 | any language |
| 18 | Does the video end within ~20 s of the payoff (*heuristic*) with one specific next step, instead of a long recap, goodbye or generic "iscriviti"? | End screens use the last 5–20 s; viewers drop at the end; abrupt endings | Y13, P2, P8 | any language |
| 19 | Are key terms mumbled, overlapped or masked by noise (likely caption errors)? | Poor audio lowers credibility and caption accuracy | R22, Y16 | any language |

Language note: every principle above is language-agnostic; only the marker inventory (check 10), the tu/voi
choice (check 13) and the example phrases are Italian-specific. Filler evidence comes from English and German,
none from Italian; English words-per-minute targets do not transfer [R12], so compare speech rate with the
speaker's own baseline.

## 5. Implications for the cut proposal

1. **Cut to the promise.** Propose removing everything before the first statement of the promise except one
   short greeting, so the promise lands within 10–20 s [Y3, Y8, Y10, P1]. If the result is strong but late,
   propose (never impose) a cold open: a 5–10-s clip of the result or the problem moved to the start [Y8, Y1].
2. **Dead air, not every pause.** Shorten silences inside speech longer than ~1 s; keep ~0.3–0.5 s at idea
   boundaries and before key terms; never butt sentences together without a breath (*heuristics*; a pause
   before a word helps it stick [R17]; avoid long silences [P5]; tool defaults: [video-editing-repos-research.md](video-editing-repos-research.md)).
3. **Retakes and fillers.** Keep the most fluent complete take: fluency drives perceived quality [R14]. Cut long
   or clustered *ehm* and tic markers when the cut lands on a word boundary; leave isolated short ones and
   markers that carry meaning [R16, R15, R26]. Over-cutting yields choppy audio and strings of jump cuts.
4. **Waits.** Speed up or cut silent agent waits; keep a second or two at 1× at each end so the viewer sees the
   agent working; keep speech at 1× (*heuristic*; comprehension falls past 2× [R11]); a speed badge is optional.
5. **Digressions.** Propose cutting tangents that do not serve the promise, marked optional with the reason
   [R8, P6]; keep caveats that change how the viewer would use the tool [R23]. No music under explanation [R9].
6. **Jump cuts.** Acceptable on YouTube; place them at sentence boundaries; avoid kept fragments under ~1–2 s
   and rapid strings of cuts visible in the webcam (*heuristic*; evidence covers only continuity cuts [R25]).
7. **Readability.** When code or text is the subject, propose a zoom or crop so that at most about 80–120
   columns span the frame [P4, P5], held long enough to read, without rapid zoom in and out; keep the webcam
   overlay and player controls off the content [P4]; viewers use phones and, in the US mostly, TVs [Y17]. A
   zoom also works as a signal [R5].
8. **Keep the demo moving, let steps land.** Every stretch should show and tell at once or be cut or sped up
   [P4]; in tutorials, keep each command and result on screen long enough to copy, because tutorial viewers
   re-watch and follow steps [R2, R1].
9. **Structure and ending.** Derive chapters from spoken signposts (00:00 first, ≥3, each ≥10 s) and never cut
   away a chapter's opening sentence [Y11, R7]; end shortly after the payoff and keep the last 5–20 s free of
   essential content that end-screen elements would cover [Y13, P2].
10. **Audio.** Flag clipping, noise, keyboard clatter and level jumps for re-recording or removal; match
    loudness across cuts [R22, Y16, P4, P5].

## 6. Weak or contested claims

- **"Keep videos under 6 minutes."** From MOOC engagement correlations [R1]; motivated learners watched far
  longer [R4]; short lecture attention limits are poorly supported (Bradbury 2016, [doi:10.1152/advan.00109.2016](https://doi.org/10.1152/advan.00109.2016));
  YouTube gives no ideal length. Length should follow the content; padding is the problem.
- **"Viewers decide in N seconds" and the "8-second attention span".** Official windows differ (5–10 s, 15 s,
  30 s: [Y10], [Y8], [Y1]); the 8-second figure has no traceable study (discussed by Bradbury 2016; a 2017 BBC
  investigation is cited by secondary sources, not fetched).
- **Retention benchmarks** such as "keep at least 60 % past 30 s" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/))
  or "you lose 20 % in 15 s" ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-intros/)) are vendor figures; the only
  official number is the ≥50 %-at-30-s cut-off of the "above typical intros" list [Y2], a list rule, not a target.
- **"Remove every ehm."** Evidence is mixed [R15], short fillers barely register [R16], markers have functions
  [R26], and no Italian data exist. Target clusters, long fillers and tics.
- **"Faster is better" and wpm targets.** The 185–254 wpm range in Brame's table carries no citation [R3]; [R1]
  is correlational; rates differ by language [R12]; comprehension holds to about 2× playback [R11].
- **"Always show your face."** Talking heads went with engagement in MOOCs [R1]; the meta-analysis finds no
  learning benefit, only more motivation and cognitive load [R24].
- **"Never say *in questo video vedremo*."** Too strong: make the topic clear early [Y8, Y9]; previews are
  signals [R6]; the harm lies in length and vagueness [P4].
- **"YouTube favours long videos / raw watch time."** Not all watch time counts the same [Y5]. The lines "as
  long as it needs to be, but no longer" (attributed to Rene Ritchie) and "recommendations are independent of
  duration" (attributed to Todd Sherman) were found only in creator blogs, not in primary sources.
- **Verbal calls to action** ("iscriviti e attiva la campanella", above all at the start): no evidence found
  either way; YouTube documents only end screens and cards; [P2] argues against announcing the end.
- **"Pattern interrupts every N seconds", constant zooms, background music.** No evidence found; coherence
  research warns against extraneous sounds and details [R9, R8]. The visual-transition peaks of [R2] are
  navigation events, not proof that transitions raise retention.
- **"Jump cuts look unprofessional."** A YouTube convention with no instructional-video evidence either way; a
  2026 marketing-conference paper on 20-s talking-head clips ([PDF](https://archives.marketing-trends-congress.com/2026/pages/PDF/paper_professor_DOST_HUANG.pdf),
  not parsed, seen only as a search summary) reportedly found seamless cuts liked more than unedited footage.
- **Transfer to YouTube tutorials.** Most studies measure learning in labs or MOOCs, not voluntary viewing,
  and the MrBeast rules [P2] are tuned for mass entertainment with unconfirmed provenance: directions usually
  agree with YouTube's own advice, magnitudes do not transfer. Treat them as hypotheses.
